four chapters.

The sports are rooted in road racing, factory competition, and national motorcycle championships. Their modern identities developed over decades.

1949–1950

Grand Prix foundations

MotoGP traces its premier class back to the first Motorcycle Grand Prix World Championship in 1949. Formula 1 began the World Drivers' Championship in 1950.

Safety, aerodynamics, electronics, turbocharging, and tyre development reshaped international competition and helped define each championship's modern identity.

Hybrid thinking grows

Advanced electronics matured, MotoGP adopted four-stroke prototypes, and the FIA World Endurance Championship returned as a unified manufacturer-led series in 2012.

Electrification arrives

Formula E began its first season in 2014. Formula 1 entered the hybrid turbo era, while road-relevant efficiency became increasingly central to endurance and open-wheel racing.

The machinery basics

Fast parts,
working together.

A racing car or bike is a package of power, grip, aerodynamics, control, and protection. Here are the systems that shape lap time.

The performance chain

Power becomes control

Engineers turn stored energy into acceleration while balancing grip, braking, aerodynamics, tyre load, and driver feedback.

01 / POWER UNIT

Engine & motor

A combustion engine burns fuel; an electric motor turns stored electrical energy into motion. Hybrid cars combine both, with the exact system set by the series.

02 / TRANSMISSION

Gears & final drive

Gear ratios match engine or motor speed to wheel speed. The transmission sends torque to the driven wheels while keeping the power unit in a useful range.

03 / AERODYNAMICS

Bodywork & airflow

Wings and bodywork guide air. Car downforce can increase cornering grip but adds drag; motorcycle aero must also work with the rider's changing position.

04 / TYRES

The contact patch

Only a small area of each tyre touches the track. Temperature, pressure, compound, wear, and surface conditions shape grip, braking, and acceleration.

05 / BRAKING

Turn speed into control

Friction brakes turn motion into heat. Hybrid and electric cars can also recover some braking energy electrically; riders rely on precise front and rear brake control.

06 / TELEMETRY

Sensors & decisions

Teams analyse speed, temperatures, pressures, position, energy, and component loads. The data helps diagnose problems and refine setup and race strategy.

07 / CAR STRUCTURE

Chassis & survival cell

The chassis holds the car together and provides mounting points for the suspension and powertrain. Top-level single-seaters and prototypes use a strong central safety cell.

Why it matters

Low mass and stiffness help the car respond consistently, while the survival cell is designed to protect the driver. WEC includes both prototype and production-derived GT constructions, so designs differ by class.

08 / MECHANICAL GRIP

Suspension & alignment

Springs, dampers, and suspension links control how the wheels move over bumps, kerbs, and during braking or cornering.

Why it matters

Setup changes affect tyre contact, ride height, stability, and driver confidence. Engineers balance a responsive car against one that remains predictable over a race distance.

09 / THERMAL CONTROL

Cooling & airflow

Radiators, ducts, and heat exchangers help keep engines, batteries, motors, brakes, and electronics within their operating ranges.

Why it matters

Cooling openings affect aerodynamic drag, so teams balance heat rejection with airflow efficiency. Traffic, ambient temperature, and race pace can change the cooling demands.

10 / DRIVER PROTECTION

Safety cell & crash structures

The cockpit safety cell and energy-absorbing structures are designed to protect the driver in an impact.

Why it matters

Safety equipment is tested against the rules of each championship. Seat restraints, head protection, barriers, and extraction procedures all contribute to safety.

07 / MOTORCYCLE CHASSIS

Frame & swingarm

The frame supports the engine and steering assembly; the swingarm carries the rear wheel. Their geometry affects stability and how the bike turns.

Why it matters

Engineers tune stiffness and geometry to suit braking, acceleration, corner entry, and tyre behaviour. The rider's body position is part of the overall balance.

08 / SUSPENSION

Forks & rear shock

Front forks and the rear shock help the tyres follow the track while controlling pitch under braking and acceleration.

Why it matters

Spring and damping settings influence grip, stability, and feedback. A setup that works on smooth corners may feel very different over bumps or under hard braking.

09 / RIDER CONTROLS

Electronics & feedback

Throttle, brakes, clutch, and gear controls connect the rider to the bike. Electronic systems help manage power delivery and engine braking.

Why it matters

Electronics can help make power more manageable, but they do not choose a line or replace rider judgement. Feedback from the tyres helps riders adjust their inputs lap by lap.

Start here · motorsport 101

A race is more
than a fast lap.

Motorsport is organised competition where people race purpose-built or modified vehicles on a defined course under shared rules.

01 · THE WEEKEND

Practice, qualify, race

Practice lets teams learn the track and tune the vehicle. Qualifying decides starting positions. The race rewards pace, decisions, consistency, and avoiding mistakes.

02 · THE GRID

Drivers, riders & teams

A competitor controls the machine; engineers and mechanics prepare it; strategists make calls. Championships may award individual and team or manufacturer titles.

03 · THE RULES

Fairness & safety

Technical rules limit vehicle design. Sporting rules cover starts, track limits, pit stops, flags, penalties, and points. Officials apply them to keep competition safe and comparable.

04 · THE PURPOSE

What does racing give us?

It tests human skill and engineering under pressure, creates shared stories for fans, and can accelerate learning about materials, safety, energy recovery, tyres, and control systems.

Every series is different: F1 is a single-seat car sprint championship; Formula E is electric single-seater racing; WEC is multi-class endurance racing; MotoGP is prototype motorcycle racing. Their regulations, vehicles, scoring, and race formats are not interchangeable.
First-timer's guide

How a race
weekend works.

Race formats differ by championship and can change between seasons. These explainers outline the common flow; official regulations decide the details.

01 · Single-seaters

Formula 1

Most events use practice, qualifying, then a Grand Prix. Selected rounds add a sprint format with its own qualifying and shorter race.

What to watch

Practice is for setup and tyre learning; qualifying sets the grid. Race strategy revolves around tyre life, pit stops, track position, weather, and safety-car timing. Points and sprint rules follow the season's sporting regulations.

FIA regulations ↗
02 · Electric street racing

Formula E

Practice and knockout qualifying lead into the E-Prix. The race's energy target and any event-specific features make pace management central.

What to watch

Qualifying duels set the starting order; drivers balance attack with energy use and regenerative braking. Attack Mode and pit-boost availability are event- and season-dependent, so check the event guide rather than assume they apply every time.

Formula E rules ↗
03 · Multi-class endurance

WEC

Practice and qualifying prepare the field for a multi-hour race. Hypercar and LMGT3 cars share the circuit while contesting class results.

What to watch

Driver crews rotate through stints. Teams manage fuel, tyres, traffic, repairs, and night or changing-weather conditions. Each car's class position matters alongside the overall order.

Official WEC season & regulations ↗
04 · Grand Prix motorcycles

MotoGP

Practice determines direct entry to the qualifying stages; qualifying sets the grid for both the sprint and Grand Prix at most events.

What to watch

The sprint is shorter than the main race, and each has its own points. Riders manage tyre choice, braking, passing, and changing grip; weather and event schedules can alter the weekend programme.

Aerodynamics · explained simply

How a wind tunnel
finds speed.

Air is invisible, but it pushes and pulls on every moving vehicle. Teams use experiments and computer models to shape airflow.

Downforce is aerodynamic force pressing a car toward the track. It helps tyres produce more cornering and braking grip, but often adds drag—the air resistance that slows the car on a straight.

Build a model

Teams test a scaled car model in a tunnel. Sensors and pressure taps measure forces and pressure around the bodywork.

Move the air

A powerful fan creates controlled airflow. A moving belt under the model imitates the road passing beneath the car and wheels.

Measure the balance

Engineers compare downforce, drag, and front-to-rear balance. Smoke or tufts can help visualise where airflow separates or becomes turbulent.

Test, then validate

Small design changes are compared, simulated, and checked on track. Regulations limit wind-tunnel and computational-fluid-dynamics testing time.

Why it matters when following a race: a high-downforce setup can be quick in corners but slower on straights; a low-drag setup may pass more easily but give up some corner grip. Dirty air from a car ahead can also reduce the following car's aerodynamic performance.
